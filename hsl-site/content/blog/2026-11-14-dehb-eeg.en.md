*Study presented at EUSIPCO 2023 by Burak Akbuğday, Özge Ada Bozbaş, Dr. Özlem Karabiber Cura (İzmir Kâtip Çelebi University), Sude Pehlivan and Prof. Dr. Aydın Akan: "Detection of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder by Using EEG Feature Maps and Deep Learning" ([doi:10.23919/EUSIPCO58844.2023.10289818](https://doi.org/10.23919/EUSIPCO58844.2023.10289818)).*

Picture a child who can't sit still in class, struggles to focus on homework and jumps to the next task before finishing the last. These behaviours can be signs of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (**ADHD**), but they can also point to tiredness, boredom or something else entirely. ADHD begins in childhood and, if it goes unnoticed and unsupported, can make daily life harder into adulthood.

Today ADHD diagnosis relies largely on the clinician's observations, on what parents and teachers report, and on questionnaires. There is no universally accepted objective measurement yet, so diagnosis depends heavily on the clinician's experience. So we asked: can brain signals offer objective support for this decision?

## Listening To The Brain's Electricity

EEG records the brain's electrical activity through electrodes on the scalp; it is painless, inexpensive and quick. The study used 4-minute EEG recordings, taken at rest with eyes open at İzmir Kâtip Çelebi University, from 15 children diagnosed with ADHD and 18 children without a diagnosis.

Many studies that try to recognise ADHD from EEG look at the power of brain waves in different frequency bands. We focused instead on six features that measure how **irregular, complex and unpredictable** the signal is. These measures, such as fractal dimensions, entropy and the Hurst exponent, describe the "texture" of brain activity.

## Turning Numbers Into Maps

Computing these features for each electrode gives a list of numbers. But that list loses an important piece of information: **where on the head** each number came from. Yet which brain regions are more active may be exactly the clue we are looking for in a condition like ADHD.

So we placed each feature on a small **head map** that follows the real layout of the electrodes. For every 5-second slice of recording, the result was a picture showing complexity across brain regions in colour. In total, around 9,500 maps were created.

Once it is a picture, an AI that is very good at looking at pictures can step in: **convolutional neural networks** (CNNs). We explained how they work in our [CNN post](/en/blog/how-does-a-cnn-work/).

{{< alinti >}}The aim is not to replace the clinician, but to offer an objective second opinion from the brain.{{< /alinti >}}

## What Did We Find?

{{< sayilar >}}
33 | children (15 with ADHD, 18 without)
~9,500 | EEG maps
92.5% | highest accuracy on unseen maps
{{< /sayilar >}}

We designed two CNN models and trained them on these maps. On maps they had not seen before, the models told children with and without ADHD apart with **88% to 92.5%** accuracy. Because the maps preserve regional information about the brain, the results were encouraging compared with similar approaches that use the numbers alone.

## Why It Matters

Such a method never aims to replace the clinician. But adding an objective second opinion from the brain to a diagnosis based on observation could help reduce the risk of misdiagnosis and reach the right support sooner. Because the recording is painless and short, it is also well suited to children.

This study was one of the first steps in a series of studies our lab carries out on ADHD and dementia with EEG maps and deep learning, and it was partially supported by IEU BAP project 2022-07.
